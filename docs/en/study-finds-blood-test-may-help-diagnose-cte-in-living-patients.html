Study Finds Blood Test May Help Diagnose CTE in Living Patients

Thursday, November 19, 2026, 21:00 UTC · 35 sources

Researchers at Boston University and Banner Health have identified a potential blood test that could diagnose chronic traumatic encephalopathy in living patients by measuring elevated levels of a specific protein, according to a study published in the Journal of the American Medical Association.

The research team analyzed blood samples from 21 former football players who donated their brains for post-mortem study. Autopsies subsequently confirmed CTE in 18 of the subjects. The study revealed that participants with high-stage CTE displayed significantly elevated concentrations of a protein called endogenously cleaved microtubule-binding region tau, or eMTBR-tau, compared to those with low-stage CTE or no CTE.

In addition, researchers found that five participants who suffered from both CTE and Alzheimer's disease exhibited the highest levels of eMTBR-tau. A separate blood marker, p-tau217, was elevated exclusively in participants with Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that blood tests could help clinicians differentiate between CTE and Alzheimer's disease during a patient's lifetime.

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy is a degenerative brain disease linked to repeated head impacts that currently can only be diagnosed posthumously. Michael Alosco, co-director of clinical research at the BU CTE Center, described the findings as a potential milestone, while emphasizing that the study is preliminary due to its small sample size and lack of age-matched controls. If larger studies validate the findings, the test could enable clinical trials for new treatments and potentially seek FDA approval within five years.
