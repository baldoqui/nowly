Food Safety Experts Outline Guidelines for Sanitizing and Maintaining Kitchen Cutting Boards

Sunday, November 22, 2026, 19:45 UTC · 5 sources

Food safety researchers and kitchen equipment specialists have detailed essential guidelines for properly sanitizing and maintaining household cutting boards, emphasizing that correct cleaning practices are more critical to preventing bacterial contamination than choosing between wood, plastic, or composite materials.

According to Donald Schaffner, a professor of food science at Rutgers University, standard washing with warm, soapy water is sufficient for everyday chopping but does not fully sanitize a board following contact with raw meat or poultry. Because tap water warm enough to kill bacteria would scald human skin, effective sanitization after preparing raw meat requires pouring boiling water over the board, using a dishwasher sanitize cycle, or applying a diluted bleach solution. Experts noted that vinegar and hydrogen peroxide solutions are significantly less effective at killing bacteria compared to bleach.

While plastic was historically viewed as the safer option due to its nonporous nature, food safety research from the University of Wisconsin-Madison demonstrated that bacteria such as salmonella and E. coli are pulled down into wood fibers where they fail to multiply and eventually die off. Conversely, plastic boards become increasingly difficult to scrub clean once deep knife cuts create grooves that trap chicken fat and bacterial residue. Nicole Papantoniou, senior director of the Kitchen & Cooking Lab at the Good Housekeeping Institute, highlighted composite boards made of resin-bonded fibers as a durable alternative, noting that knife cuts on composite remain open for easier washing.

Maintaining wooden boards requires routine care to ensure longevity and prevent structural breakdown. Lisa Bosio, vice president of group marketing at butcher block manufacturer John Boos & Co., recommends applying a light layer of food-safe mineral oil monthly to prevent wood from drying out. Food analysts also advise drying wood boards upright on their edges to allow air circulation across both sides, using a baking soda paste to scrub away stubborn odors, and applying a three-percent hydrogen peroxide solution to lighten organic stains from produce like tomatoes or beets.

Experts recommend replacing cutting boards whenever they can no longer be visibly cleaned or maintained safely. Plastic boards should be discarded when deep grooves or plastic curls form that could transfer into food, while wooden boards should be replaced if they crack, warp, or develop dark spots indicative of mold.
