FCC Approves Rule Allowing State Prisons to Jam Contraband Cellphones

Thursday, November 19, 2026, 21:00 UTC · 5 sources

The Federal Communications Commission voted unanimously on Thursday to allow state prisons across the United States to jam signals from contraband cellphones, granting corrections officials a long-sought tool to combat illegal inmate communications.

Under the new rule, state corrections agencies will not automatically receive authority to jam signals. Instead, departments must submit detailed facility-specific plans to the FCC and conduct testing to prove that jamming will not disrupt legitimate wireless services, emergency response calls, or home security systems outside prison perimeters. The decision removes a nearly century-old restriction that previously restricted signal-jamming technology exclusively to federal facilities. Law enforcement officials estimate that approximately 25,000 contraband cellphones are confiscated each year in state and local prisons, where inmates use smuggled devices to run drug operations, coordinate prison riots, and arrange attacks on correctional staff. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr described the rule as a major new tool for law enforcement, while inmate rights advocates argued that high official prison phone rates push inmates to acquire contraband devices to stay in touch with families.

South Carolina corrections directors have spearheaded national efforts to obtain jamming permissions following years of security incidents linked to smuggled cellphones, including a 2010 attack on a corrections officer and a fatal 2018 prison riot. Controlled tests conducted at South Carolina prisons in 2008 and 2019 demonstrated that targeted jamming frequencies could disable devices within prison auditoriums while leaving outside cellular service unaffected. The state's Broad River Correctional Institution is expected to be among the first facilities to apply for FCC authorization.
