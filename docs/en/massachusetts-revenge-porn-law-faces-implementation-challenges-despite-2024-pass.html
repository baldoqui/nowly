Massachusetts Revenge Porn Law Faces Implementation Challenges Despite 2024 Passage

Sunday, November 22, 2026, 04:45 UTC · 5 sources

Victims of nonconsensual pornography in Massachusetts report ongoing difficulties in seeking justice, citing a lack of familiarity and proper training among law enforcement officers following the enactment of state legislation in 2024.

Advocates and victims indicate that reporting nonconsensual explicit content often results in administrative confusion and delayed investigations. Katelynn Spencer, an advocate who supported the 2024 law after a case against her perpetrator was dismissed due to a lack of existing statutes, expressed disappointment with the current enforcement. Another victim reported having to present a printed copy of the newly passed law to local police officers after they initially expressed unfamiliarity with the statute, leading to months of delays before charges were filed against his ex-girlfriend.

Stephanie Holt, deputy director of operations at the Victim Rights Law Center, stated that victims frequently experience a lack of guidance when reporting to authorities, workplace officials, or academic institutions. Holt highlighted the necessity of comprehensive law enforcement training to ensure proper evidence collection and statutory execution, advising victims to retain all digital communication as evidence.

Under Massachusetts law, a first offense for sharing nonconsensual explicit images carries a maximum penalty of 2.5 years in jail and a $10,000 fine, while a second offense is classified as a felony punishable by up to 10 years in state prison. Massachusetts Governor Maura Healey, who signed the legislation into law, stated that she expects enforcing entities to fully implement the statute to hold perpetrators accountable.
