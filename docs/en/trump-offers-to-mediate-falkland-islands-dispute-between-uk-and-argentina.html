Trump Offers to Mediate Falkland Islands Dispute Between UK and Argentina

Sunday, September 13, 2026, 00:07 UTC

U.S. President Donald Trump offered to mediate the sovereignty dispute over the Falkland Islands between the United Kingdom and Argentina, warning during a visit to Ireland that rising diplomatic friction over the South Atlantic territory could lead to a potential disaster.

Speaking to reporters in Dublin following a meeting with Irish Prime Minister Micheál Martin, Trump questioned whether London would be willing or able to deploy military resources to defend the remote archipelago given domestic burdens, including energy issues and immigration. According to GB News, Trump expressed confidence that he could resolve the conflict if asked by both nations, noting the significant geographical distance requiring weeks of maritime travel. The Argentine government under President Javier Milei welcomed Trump's remarks as a positive development, with officials in Buenos Aires reviewing potential diplomatic avenues ahead of a planned bilateral meeting at the United Nations General Assembly. The renewed rhetoric follows Argentina's recent implementation of legal and administrative sanctions against foreign firms involved in the Sea Lion offshore oil development project surrounding the islands. In response, a British government spokesperson affirmed that UK armed forces remain on continuous alert to safeguard the territory, emphasizing that sovereignty remains non-negotiable and dependent on the self-determination of the islanders.

Argentina has claimed sovereignty over the Falkland Islands, known in Spanish as Las Malvinas, since British forces established control in 1833. The long-standing territorial conflict escalated into an armed conflict in 1982, ending after 74 days with the defeat of Argentine forces and the deaths of 649 Argentine military members, 255 British troops, and three civilians. Although diplomatic relations were re-established in 1990, the United Nations continues to recognize the area as a disputed territory, while London points to a 2013 referendum where 99.8 percent of island residents voted to maintain their status as a British Overseas Territory.
